How RAG Answers From Your Docs

The model was never trained on aem.live and never will be. It answers your questions by reading the relevant paragraphs in the prompt, every single time. That one sentence is the whole idea.

The short version

It is search, then paste, then ask

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation, which is a heavy name for three plain steps:

  • Retrieval — find the handful of paragraphs most likely to hold the answer.
  • Augmented — paste those paragraphs into the prompt, above the question.
  • Generation — the model writes an answer using only what it just read.

There is no training, no fine-tuning, and no moment where the model “learns” your documentation. Nothing about the model changes. You are just very good at deciding what to show it.

The thing that surprises people

If you paste the right paragraph into a prompt by hand and ask your question underneath it, you have done RAG. Everything else on this page is machinery for doing that automatically, 583 chunks at a time, in under a second.

The one wire that breaks everything

The question must be turned into numbers by the same embedding model used on the chunks. Swap the model on one side and the numbers stop meaning the same thing — search still returns five chunks, they are just the wrong five, with no error anywhere. If you change embedding models, rebuild the whole store.

Where your cleaning work shows up

Look at the url, title and path lines on each source. Those come from dataset.jsonl. Without them the model gets five anonymous walls of text and cannot cite anything — which is the whole reason stage 2 reads the JSONL and not the Markdown folder.

Worth knowing

Where this goes wrong

Every RAG failure is one of four things, and none of them are the model's fault:

  • The right chunk was never retrieved. The most common failure by far. The model answered honestly from four irrelevant chunks. Fix retrieval, not the prompt.
  • The chunk was retrieved but cut badly — a table row split from its headings, so the words arrived without their meaning. This is exactly what flattening the box-art tables prevents.
  • The answer is spread across many pages. Five chunks cannot answer “summarise every deprecation in 2026”. Retrieval finds passages; it does not survey a corpus.
  • Nothing in your docs answers it — and the model should say so. That is what the “if the sources do not contain the answer, say so” instruction is for.
How to test it

Write 20 real questions with the answer's URL next to each. Then measure one number: how often the correct page appears in the top 5. That single percentage tells you more than any amount of prompt tuning, because if the right chunk never arrives, no prompt can save the answer.
